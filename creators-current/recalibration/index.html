Recalibration: Reset, Refocus, Return

Sometimes the instrument drifts and you need to return to the reference before moving forward.

The pause in Creator's Current was not silence. It was bandwidth management.

From 2021 onward, I spent time working in corporate contexts that gave me real skills, real speed, and real exposure to how larger organisations function. The work was useful. But somewhere in the process, the urgent crowded out the essential. The newsletter was not the only thing that got quieter. Other things did too.

This issue is about what happened after I noticed that, and what came out of deciding to address it directly.

Reset

The first thing I had to be honest about was that busyness had become a hiding place.

When you are always occupied, you do not have to sit with the harder questions. Am I building something I actually believe in? Am I where I said I wanted to be? Is the way I am working aligned with what I said mattered to me?

I had drifted. Not dramatically, not in a way anyone outside would have noticed. But internally, I had allowed the pace of employment and obligation to become a substitute for direction. Keeping moving felt like progress even when the movement was largely circular.

I also had to admit something I had been avoiding: I was afraid of building something under my own name. A job provides cover. When something goes wrong, it is "the project" or "the company." When you build independently, your name is on everything. That felt heavier than I had acknowledged.

The reset meant putting those things on the table honestly. Not to dramatise them. Just to see them clearly.

Refocus

Refocusing meant returning to what I had said mattered most and being serious about acting accordingly.

My Bible-based values had always been the stated priority. But in practice, Bible reading, prayer, rest, and spiritual engagement had been consistently deprioritised in favour of what was urgent. The logic was always "later." Later never arrived.

So refocusing meant restructuring the day so that the things I said were most important actually happened first, not in the cracks.

It also meant leaving the 9-to-5 model. Not because employment is wrong. But because it had become a structure that was making it harder, not easier, to live the way I wanted to live. The stability it offered was real. But the constraints it imposed on how I spent my time, my energy, and my attention were also real, and they were adding up.

Presence over omnipresence was another part of this. I had been trying to be available for everything and everyone, which meant I was fully present for almost nothing. Refocusing required making actual choices about where my attention went rather than spreading it thin across everything that asked for it.

Return

Recalibration, as the word suggests, means returning to a fixed point of reference.

Think about what calibration actually involves. At the Bureau of Standards Jamaica, instruments are tested and adjusted against known standards. A scale that has drifted slightly from accuracy is not thrown out. It is compared to a reliable reference, then corrected to align with it again.

That is what this phase of return looked like for me. Not a reinvention. Not a dramatic pivot. A return to the things I already knew were right: spiritual activity, genuine rest, time with the people who matter, work that is honest and useful, and a recommitment to keeping them central rather than marginal.

Returning also meant accepting that the process had to be ongoing. Drift is natural. It happens to instruments. It happens to people. What matters is whether correction is chosen when drift is noticed.

What this looks like in practice

For different people, recalibration looks different. But the pattern tends to be similar:

For a brand leader who has been performing rather than building: the reset is admitting that the audience they have built does not match the work they actually want to do. The refocus is getting honest about what that work is. The return is producing it slowly, clearly, without pretending it is bigger than it is.

For an independent designer who has been taking every project: the reset is recognising that constant availability is not generosity, it is avoidance. The refocus is defining what kind of work actually develops their craft. The return is saying no to the rest, even before the good work arrives.

For a social media manager who is exhausted by the pace: the reset is accepting that the current pace is not sustainable and that something has to give. The refocus is identifying which parts of the work are genuinely energising. The return is redesigning the role or the job around those.

For an online seller who has been growing but losing clarity: the reset is pausing to audit what is actually working. The refocus is cutting what is not. The return is doing the good things better, with less noise around them.

For a burnt-out creator: the reset is acknowledging the exhaustion without apology. The refocus is remembering why the work started. The return is beginning again, smaller, slower, and with better boundaries than before.

I am neurodivergent, and part of this recalibration involved designing my days with that reality in mind rather than pretending it did not exist. Working in ways that suit my actual cognitive patterns rather than the patterns I wished I had. That was its own form of return to honesty about how I actually function.

Key takeaway

Accuracy over activity. Drift is natural. Correction is chosen. You do not have to wait for everything to fall apart before you return to what matters. The earlier you notice the drift, the smaller the correction needs to be.
