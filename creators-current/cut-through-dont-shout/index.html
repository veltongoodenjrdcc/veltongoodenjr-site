Cut Through, Don't Shout: The Power of Minimal Signals

Small brands rarely win by being louder. They win by becoming sharper, clearer, and more intentional about the signals they send.

Small brands and independent creators face a real attention problem. Not because nobody is interested in what they do, but because the noise is so relentless that being heard at all feels like an impossible task.

The standard advice: post more, be louder, go harder. But that strategy is designed for players who can sustain it. Most small operators cannot. And even when they try, they end up exhausted and invisible, drowned out by the brands with bigger budgets and bigger teams doing the same thing, just at a higher volume.

There is a better way to think about this. It comes from biology and economics, not marketing.

The noise problem

Herbert Simon, the economist and cognitive scientist, identified something important back in 1971 that has only become more relevant since: in an information-rich world, the thing that becomes scarce is not information. It is attention.

We are now so overloaded with messages, content, and claims that most of it disappears. Our brains have developed a kind of nose blindness to it, the same way you stop noticing a smell after a while. Constant exposure creates immunity. The louder everyone shouts, the more effectively people tune out.

So shouting louder is not the answer. What is?

Signalling theory and why it matters for small brands

Signalling theory, rooted in evolutionary biology, gives us a useful framework. The idea is simple: signals work when they are both credible (believable, based on something real) and efficient (worth the cost being paid to send them).

A peacock's tail is the classic example. It is expensive to grow and maintain. Which is precisely why it signals something genuine: only a bird with real genetic fitness can afford to carry it. The cost makes the signal credible.

For brands, the equivalent is not necessarily money. It is specificity, effort, and the kind of attention to detail that costs time and care. Those things are hard to fake at scale, which is why they carry weight.

The CardBuddy example

Sam Feldman was a University of Maryland student who built a card-holder business called CardBuddy. The company was small. The product was straightforward. He did not have a marketing budget that could compete with the big players in his category.

But he did three things that created a disproportionate signal:

He personally hand-signed 12,000 thank-you notes to customers. He inserted a dollar bill into one in every five orders, at a cost of about 20 cents per unit. And when products were defective or customers were unhappy, he replaced them generously, sometimes sending ten new wallets including premium leather ones when a customer reported a problem.

The result: CardBuddy received 63 mentions of the word "service" per 1,000 Amazon reviews, compared to two to four for competitors in the same category. The business grew into a six-figure operation while Feldman was still an undergraduate. He won the Student Entrepreneur of the Year award in 2016.

This is minimal signalling working. Not the loudest brand. Not the most omnipresent one. The one that sent a credible, efficient signal to exactly the people who mattered: real customers with a real reason to trust the business and tell others.

What nature already knows

Birds figured this out long before we did. Certain species do not win mates by making the most noise. They win by being strategic about placement, by timing their calls when they carry the furthest, by offering something meaningful rather than just persisting louder than everyone else.

The shrewd bird reads the room. Conserves energy. Chooses the moment. Makes the gesture count.

That is the model for small brands operating in competitive spaces. Not omnipresence. Presence with purpose.

What to actually do

Three practical moves that follow from this:

Audit your current signals. Look at your bio, your website, your recent posts. Do they send a credible signal about who you are and what you actually do? Or are they vague, generic, or cluttered? Start with clarity before you add volume.

Define your emotional core. What is the one thing you want people to feel or understand after encountering your brand? That is your signal. Everything else is noise. When you are clear on that, you can start making choices instead of just producing output.

Do one high-impact thing per week. Not ten mediocre things. One thing that costs you genuine effort or care: a personal response, a genuinely useful piece of content, a specific gesture to someone who deserves it. Small brands win one person at a time. That is not a weakness. That is leverage.

Key takeaway

The goal is not to reach the most people. It is to reach the right people with something credible and worth their attention. Minimal signals that are specific and honest will outperform louder ones that are generic and forgettable.
